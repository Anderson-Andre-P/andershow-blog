## Why I built it

A good part of my work is on apps that depend on location: background tracking, arriving at a stop, entering and leaving a geofence, calculating speed. Testing that is tedious. Either you go out walking or driving with the phone, or you use a fake location app that teleports the device from one point to another, with nothing like the behavior of a real GPS.

I wanted a tool that would make the phone **believe** it was traveling a real route, at the right speed, with the imperfections of a real GPS, and keep working with the screen off, which is exactly when background location bugs show up. Since I couldn't find one that did all of that, I built the **GPS Simulator**.

This post was updated a day after the first version. In the meantime the app gained a floating panel with a joystick, four home screen widgets (one of them with a live map), five languages and a lot of interface polish. The new sections start halfway through.

## What it does

It's an Android app, for Android 12 or newer, built with Flutter. You mark points on the map, build the route and start the simulation. From then on, any other app on the device that asks for the location gets the simulated positions.

- **Real routes:** the route can come from OSRM, GraphHopper, your own server or be a straight line. You can also search for an address and use it as a point, import routes in GPX, KML or GeoJSON and export or share them in GPX and KML.
- **One way, round trip or loop**, with the return leg routed following the direction of the streets.
- **Six vehicles**, each with its own default speed: car, motorcycle, bus, truck, bicycle and on foot.
- **Saved routes that work offline**, with rename, duplicate, export and point-by-point editing.
- **GPS imperfections:** ready-made profiles (City, Highway and Covered area) or noise, speed variation and turns tuned by hand. A stationary point drifts like a real parked phone instead of sitting on a single coordinate.
- **Tunnels and altitude from the real map**, without a single invented number.
- **Geofence scenario**, to simulate entering an area, waiting inside it and leaving.
- **Background:** the simulation keeps running with the screen off, with a notification that shows the progress and has pause and stop buttons.
- **Floating panel** on top of any app, with a joystick, speed controls and jumps to favorite places.
- **Four home screen widgets**, including one with the map and the position moving in real time.
- **Five languages**, with regional variants, and a light, dark or system theme.
- **Help inside the app:** searchable FAQ, privacy policy and terms of use.

## Saved routes and point editing

Saving a route stores the points, the geometry, the vehicle, the speed and the mode, so it **can be simulated offline**, without asking the routing service again. Opening a saved route shows the distance and time of each direction (out only, back only or both), the stretch with altitude and tunnels and the choices made. Each route's menu has open on the map, **simulate now** from point A, rename, duplicate, export as GPX, share as GPX or KML and delete.

Points can also be adjusted one by one. Tapping a marker shows its latitude, longitude and distance from point A, and lets you move it earlier or later in the order, add a point near it or remove it. When the points change, the route is flagged as out of date until you build it again. Clearing everything asks first and leaves saved routes untouched.

## Publishing positions through Android itself

Android has an official mechanism for this: **test providers**. With Developer options enabled and the app selected as the "mock location app", it can register fake location providers and publish positions to them.

Flutter doesn't expose that API, so I wrote a small Kotlin plugin. It registers one test provider for GPS and another for the network, with the same properties as a real provider:

```kotlin
val properties = ProviderProperties.Builder()
    .setHasNetworkRequirement(network)
    .setHasSatelliteRequirement(!network)
    .setHasAltitudeSupport(true)
    .setHasSpeedSupport(true)
    .setHasBearingSupport(true)
    .setPowerUsage(ProviderProperties.POWER_USAGE_LOW)
    .setAccuracy(ProviderProperties.ACCURACY_FINE)
    .build()
locationManager.addTestProvider(name, properties)
locationManager.setTestProviderEnabled(name, true)
```

At each step of the simulation, the app builds a `Location` with latitude, longitude, speed, bearing, accuracy and time, and publishes it with `setTestProviderLocation`. The plugin also detects whether Developer options are enabled, and the app shows the steps to set it up as the mock location app.

## The rule: no invented data

The first version published a fixed altitude and lost the signal at regular intervals, anywhere on the route. They looked realistic, but they were made-up numbers. An app under test could pass because of data no real phone would ever see.

So I set a rule for the realism features: **anything that looks real must come from real map data. When there's no data, the app publishes nothing instead of inventing it.**

### Altitude and tunnels from the map

When a route is created, wherever it came from, the app sends its geometry to a **Valhalla** server:

- The `/height` endpoint returns the **terrain height** along the route, sampled every 30 meters.
- The `/trace_attributes` endpoint map-matches the route and says which stretches are **tunnels, bridges or covered areas**, based on OpenStreetMap.

With that:

- **Inside a tunnel, the GPS loses its signal**, like a real phone, and gets it back at the exit.
- **On bridges, the altitude follows the deck**, not the valley floor the terrain model has underneath it. The same goes for tunnels, relative to the hilltop above. On those stretches, the height is interpolated between the two ends.
- If the imported file already has an altitude for every point, the app uses the file's.
- **Without data**, for example offline or with the server down, the position is published **without altitude**, and the app says so on screen.

```kotlin
// No altitude is better than an invented one: without it the
// Location simply reports hasAltitude() == false.
val msl = (fix["altitude"] as Number?)?.toDouble()
if (msl != null) applyAltitude(location, msl, vertical)
```

The terrain profile is saved with the route, so the server is queried once per route, not once per simulation.

### The geoid detail

This is where a subtle problem showed up. The terrain model gives the height **above mean sea level**. A real GPS chip reports the height **above the WGS84 ellipsoid**, a mathematical surface that doesn't match sea level. In Brazil, the difference is in the tens of meters. Publishing the terrain height directly would be a plausible number, but a wrong one.

On Android 14 or newer, the system has `AltitudeConverter`, which knows that difference. The plugin runs a "probe": it creates a position at zero height on the ellipsoid and asks the converter for the matching height above sea level. The value that comes back, with its sign flipped, is the difference at that point. It's computed off the main thread and cached per area, and the published altitude matches what a real GPS would report. On older versions, which don't have the converter, the app publishes the height above sea level and says so on screen.

### Respecting public servers

By default, terrain comes from the public Valhalla server run by the FOSSGIS community, which asks for at most one request per second and for apps not to hard-code its address. The client respects that limit, the address can be changed in the settings, with a button to test the server, and the required attribution is shown on screen.

The same goes for the map. The default is Google Maps, with a standard, satellite, hybrid or terrain map, plus traffic and place icons. You can switch to OpenStreetMap, which needs no key and whose usage policy is also respected.

For Google Maps to work, I had to set up the key in the [Google Cloud Console](https://console.cloud.google.com/): create a project, enable the **Maps SDK for Android** and generate an API key for the app. Without it, the SDK draws only an empty background and logs an authorization error (`Authorization failure`). It's a simple setup, but easy to forget when running the app on a new machine, which is exactly why the key-free OpenStreetMap option is useful.

## Controlling the simulation on top of the app under test

During a test, the screen belongs to the app being tested, not to the simulator. Going back to the simulator just to pause or change the speed breaks the flow of the test. So the simulator gained a **floating panel**: a small window you can drag around, shown on top of any app while a simulation runs. It has:

- the state of the simulation, the route and the progress;
- pause, resume and stop;
- faster and slower buttons, in steps of 5 km/h;
- a **joystick** to move the position by hand, in any direction;
- the favorite places, to **jump** to one of them with a tap.

The panel collapses into a bubble and hides when the simulator itself is on screen, so it never gets in the way.

It's written in Kotlin, in a `TYPE_APPLICATION_OVERLAY` window, not in Flutter. A second Flutter engine just for the panel would be heavy and would complicate talking to the simulation, which already runs in its own isolate inside the foreground service. Instead, the panel talks to the service through the same path the notification buttons already used, an app-internal broadcast:

```kotlin
fun send(context: Context, command: String) {
    context.sendBroadcast(
        Intent("onNotificationButtonPressed")
            .setPackage(context.packageName)
            .putExtra("intentData", command),
    )
}
```

Since that channel carries a single word, the new commands became text: `faster`, `slower`, `joy:90:60` (bearing in degrees and push in percent) and `jump:-23.55:-46.63`. On the Dart side, the movement is computed by the same simulation engine, which still doesn't depend on Flutter or Android. The first push of the joystick takes the position off the route line, right where the vehicle is, and from then on each step moves in the chosen direction at the chosen share of the speed:

```dart
SimulationProgress steer(SimulationProgress p, double bearing, double power) {
  final push = power.clamp(0.0, 1.0);
  if (p.status != SimulationStatus.teleport && push == 0) return p;
  final free = p.status == SimulationStatus.teleport ? p : _leaveLine(p);
  return free.copyWith(
    joystickBearing: (bearing % 360 + 360) % 360,
    joystickPower: push,
  );
}
```

The joystick produces many moves per second, so the panel sends at most one every 150 ms, and the service only moves the position on the next simulation step. Letting go of the joystick is always sent right away.

The panel needs the "Display over other apps" permission, which lives on a screen of Android itself. Before sending the person there, the app explains in a dialog what it's for. That became a rule for every permission in the app: location, notifications, battery and overlay. Android's permission prompt says what is being asked, never why.

The map widget runs into an Android limit: a home screen widget can't run a real map. It only shows ready-made images and text, through `RemoteViews`. The solution was to draw the picture in Kotlin at each simulation step: the app fetches the OpenStreetMap tiles of the area, draws the route, the start, the end and the position with an arrow pointing where it's heading, and hands the bitmap to the widget.

A few things this approach required:

- **Map tiles are downloaded once and kept for 7 days**, with at most two downloads at a time and a User-Agent that identifies the app, as the OpenStreetMap usage policy asks. The "© OpenStreetMap" credit sits in the corner of the picture.
- **The picture goes through a binder**, which has a size limit. So it's drawn in `RGB_565` with a pixel cap, and it loses a little sharpness when the widget is stretched to fill the whole screen.
- **Drawing runs off the main thread**, and only the latest request is drawn. Text and picture reach the widget together, so the map never blinks empty every second.
- **A short route shows in full.** On a long route, the map follows the position up close.

## Realism and server settings

The settings gather everything that changes how the simulated GPS behaves, each with a short explanation underneath:

- **Tick interval:** from 0.5 s to 5 s, with 1 s as the default. Each tick carries latitude, longitude, speed, bearing, accuracy and time.
- **Test providers:** the GPS and network providers can be switched on and off separately.
- **Accuracy profile:** City, Highway or Covered area, which set noise and speed variation at once.
- **Position noise:** none, low, medium or high, and the published accuracy follows the choice.
- **Speed variation and slowing down on curves**, each with its own switch.
- **Default speeds:** one per vehicle, adjustable with plus and minus and with a restore button.
- **Routing service:** OSRM, GraphHopper or your own server, with a button to test the connection. If the service doesn't respond, the app offers the straight-line path, which doesn't depend on it.
- **Terrain server** for altitude and tunnels, with the public server's usage limit notice and the required attribution.
- **Map, units, start city, theme and language**, plus the floating panel switch.

## Five languages, without missing a string

The app speaks Brazilian Portuguese, US English, Spanish from Spain, French from France and Italian, plus three regional variants: Mexican Spanish, Canadian English and Québec French. The language follows the phone's and can be changed in the settings, from a list with each country's flag.

Instead of translation files, the copy lives in Dart classes. The Portuguese class defines every string, and each language implements it:

```dart
base class SimulatorCopyEn with SimulatorHelpCopyEn implements SimulatorCopy {
  @override
  String get buildRoute => 'Build route';
  // ...
}
```

Because each language **implements** the Portuguese class, a missing translation is a compile error, not a blank string found in production. The regional variants extend their base language and change only what the region says differently: "carro" and "celular" in Mexico, "favourite" and "metre" in Canada, and in Québec no space before the question mark, which French from France uses.

Units follow the language too: miles in US English and kilometers everywhere else. The suggested start cities are from the chosen language's country.

A widget test renders the main screens in every language and fails if any text is cut off. French and Italian tend to be the longest, and the rule is to shorten the copy, never the test.

## How it's built

The app was created from the same template I use for Flutter apps at work: **feature-based Clean Architecture**, **BLoC** for all state, `get_it` for dependency injection, `go_router` for navigation and a `Result` in use cases instead of exceptions. The simulation logic, the engine that computes each position, lives entirely in the domain layer, with no dependency on Flutter or Android, which makes it easy to test. The joystick and the jump to a place went into that same engine.

Some packages that do the heavy lifting:

- `flutter_foreground_task` to keep the simulation alive with the screen off.
- `google_maps_flutter` and `flutter_map` for the two map types.
- `home_widget` to bring the simulation data to the widgets.
- `sembast` to store routes, favorites and settings.

The native Kotlin side grew: the test provider plugin, the floating panel with its joystick and the map drawing for the widgets. The project has more than 750 tests, covering the use cases, the BLoCs, the background service, the screens in every language and in both themes, and Android's native strings. The app's summary is on its [project page](/projects/gps-simulator).

## What I learned

- **Internal tools are worth the investment.** A good simulator turns a half-hour field test into a few taps, and makes cases testable that would be hard to reproduce on the street, like entering a tunnel in the middle of a route.
- **Fake realism is worse than no realism.** An invented altitude can make a test pass by mistake. Publishing "no altitude" is more honest and more useful.
- **Geographic data hides details.** Sea level and the ellipsoid look like the same thing until you compare against a real GPS.
- **The tool has to be where the test happens.** The floating panel and the widgets exist because, during a test, the screen belongs to the app under test, not to the simulator.
- **Not everything has to be Flutter.** The panel and the map drawing turned out simpler and lighter in Kotlin, talking to Dart over a channel that already existed.
- **Let the compiler chase the translations.** With the copy in classes that implement the Portuguese one, an incomplete language doesn't even compile.
- **Public servers have rules.** Throttling requests, caching what was downloaded, letting users change the server and showing attribution are part of using a free service well.
